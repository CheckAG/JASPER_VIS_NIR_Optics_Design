What the spots tell you

The cylinder flattens the field

The limiter was the camera's dispersion-plane field curvature — best focus swung ~2 mm across the band. A plano-cylindrical N-BK7 flattener (f ≈ −70 mm) near the detector corrects it, bringing the response to ~1.9 nm mean / 2.9 nm worst.

Why cylindrical, not spherical

Only the dispersion axis sets resolution, so the corrector needs power in that plane only. A cylinder flattens the tangential focal surface without touching the slit-height axis — avoiding the whole-spectrum magnification that makes a spherical flattener a wash on a nearly-full detector.

Band edges are the residual

500 and 1000 nm focus deepest, so they carry the last of the curvature (~2.9 nm) while mid-band sits near 1 nm. The 6° detector tilt balances the two ends; a single cylinder cannot remove the small chromatic remainder.

Geometry-limited

RMSx is far larger than the ~4 µm Airy disk at f/4.9, so diffraction is negligible; the blur is ray aberration plus the residual defocus the tilt cannot remove.

The path to ~1 nm

Each wavelength reaches ~0.5 nm at its own focus, and best focus is a smooth parabola in wavelength. A 2-exposure focus bracket (detector at two positions ~1.7 mm apart, stitched) reaches ~1 nm — demonstrated in simulation, pending build.

Not shown: diffraction

BeamFour is geometric — the Airy circle is computed analytically (1.22·λ·f/#). For the true PSF at band centre you'd need a physical-optics tool.
